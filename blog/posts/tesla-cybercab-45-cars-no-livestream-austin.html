45 Cybercabs, No Livestream: What Tesla Actually Delivered in Austin

Tesla teased a livestream for the Cybercab launch, and it never happened. Here is the real story: 45 authorized cars, the same FSD software, and a rollout dressed up as a reveal.

Tesla teased a live stream for its Cybercab launch, and people waited. There were over two million views' worth of anticipation on X. The stream never happened. So what actually took place in Austin? Not the hype version, the real one.

What actually happened

Tesla's Cybercab is a two-seat robotaxi with no steering wheel and no pedals, first shown in October 2024. In early September 2026, it officially started carrying paying passengers in Austin, the first time real customers, not employees or invited testers, rode in one.

The big public event people expected stayed closed: invite-only, 21 and up, IDs checked at the door. In practice, the launch was a quiet rollout dressed up as a big reveal.

The number that matters

As of the most recent count, only 45 Cybercabs are authorized for commercial use in all of Texas. Three days earlier, that number was seven. Forty-five cars is not a fleet, at least not yet.

That figure says more than any keynote moment could have. The distance between the ambition and the current reality is measured in dozens, not thousands.

What is genuinely new, and what isn't

The hardware really is new. The Cybercab has zero manual controls, so there is no fallback if the software hiccups. That is a different bet than Waymo's approach, which retrofits normal consumer cars with sensors instead of building something purpose-made.

The software, though, is the same FSD stack already powering Tesla's Model Y robotaxis in Austin. The autonomy isn't new. Only the shell around it is.

Pricing is still unconfirmed. The rumored under-$30,000 figure that has circulated for two years wasn't nailed down either. There is no order book, no delivery timeline, and nothing concrete for individual buyers.

Smooth ride versus the future of transportation

Early riders, including Tesla commentator Sawyer Merritt, who was there, described smooth performance. That is real and worth something. But a smooth ride in a new car and the future of transportation is here are two very different claims, and only one of them happened.

The production numbers deserve the same scrutiny. The 5,000-vehicle figure that has circulated was always a ceiling rather than a target. Tesla's own engineer reportedly told regulators the company expects to field around 2,500 within the year.

Nobody in this race has a clean story

Tesla's biggest rival isn't having an easy time either. Waymo recalled 4,000 vehicles in June over construction-zone risk, and 3,800 more in May after cars stalled in standing water. Meanwhile Zoox, Amazon's robotaxi arm, is quietly expanding into Houston and San Diego without anywhere near the media attention Tesla gets for every move.

That is the honest state of robotaxis in September 2026: real progress, real setbacks, and a lot more hype than any single company can currently back up.

The honest version

Forty-five cars, no public live stream, and the same self-driving software as before. Is that a disappointment, or an honest first step dressed up in too much hype? I would genuinely like to know what you think, so drop it in the comments. I will keep tracking this as the fleet actually scales.
